The Job

Three things in one pan-off session: drain & refill ATF, replace the ATF filter, and swap the SLT solenoid from a donor U241E into the installed transaxle.

The filter is an internal, pan-off part on this transaxle — which is exactly why book time is 1.9 h for fluid + filter vs. 1.1 h for fluid alone. Since you're already in the valve body, the solenoid swap is nearly free labor.

Donor solenoids

Solenoids can be replaced new or reused from a donor transaxle — provided the donor unit had no solenoid-related faults. Test any incoming part before installing it (see Checks and Bulb Test).

A solenoid from a transaxle that failed mechanically (for example, a failure to engage a gear, with no codes stored) is a reasonable donor. A solenoid from a unit that failed electrically is not.

Do this before you drop the pan

The SLT circuit is testable from outside the transaxle at the B20 transmission wire connector. Car on the ground, no fluid spilled. If it reads bad, you've confirmed the solenoid before starting.

Read this carefully though. P2714 is a performance code — the ECM saw a turbine-vs-output speed difference, meaning clutch slip, not a broken wire. So a good resistance reading does not clear the diagnosis. A bad reading confirms the solenoid. It's a one-way test — cheap to run, and 10 minutes to potentially redirect the whole job.
Resistance specs — all U241E solenoids

Measured at 20 °C (68 °F).

SolenoidTest pointsSpec
SL1terminal 1 – 25.0 – 5.6 Ω
SL2terminal 1 – 25.0 – 5.6 Ω
SLTterminal 1 – 25.0 – 5.6 Ω
DSLconnector – solenoid body11 – 13 Ω
S4connector – solenoid body11 – 15 Ω
Mind the wiring difference. SL1/SL2/SLT are two-pin — you measure across terminals 1 and 2. DSL and S4 have one pin and ground through the case, so you measure connector-to-body. Probing a DSL/S4 style part terminal-to-terminal will read open and look "bad" when it's fine.
Reading the numbers
  • In spec — coil is electrically healthy. Code cause is hydraulic or mechanical.
  • Open / OL — broken coil winding. Dead.
  • Much higher — corroded or heat-damaged winding. Replace.
  • Below ~5 Ω — partially shorted turns. Replace.
  • Temperature matters — a cold solenoid reads low. Test at room temp for a fair comparison.
Best practice: measure the incoming donor SLT and the installed one back to back, same meter, same temperature. Relative comparison beats absolute numbers when you're deciding which to keep.
The setup

One lead from the battery positive post to one bulb terminal. This is the red path in the diagram.

Other bulb terminal to terminal 2 of the solenoid connector. Current now runs through the bulb into the coil.
3

The blue return. Touch it to the negative post and you complete the circuit.
Touch-and-release, don't hold. Each touch should give a crisp click. Release, touch again — you're checking repeatability, not endurance.
What you're looking for
GOOD: the valve moves and makes an audible click each time you touch the lead.

The intermittent case is worth calling out. Your code is intermittent under hard acceleration — that's exactly the failure a momentary bench test catches and a resistance reading misses.
Why the bulb — don't skip it

SLT is a linear PWM solenoid at roughly 5.3 Ω. Straight across a 12.6 V battery that's ~2.4 A and ~30 W into a coil the ECM only ever drives at duty cycle. Hold it on straight DC and you can cook the winding you're trying to test.

The bulb is a current limiter — series resistance that drops the voltage so the valve still strokes but the coil survives. That's the entire reason the FSM specifies it.

OptionNotes
1156 / 1157 bulbBest. 1156 ≈ 27 W; 1157 also ≈ 27 W on the bright filament
3156 / 3157 bulbElectrically identical to 1156/1157 (both ~27 W). Base differs: plastic wedge instead of bayonet
Bench supply w/ current limitCleanest if you have one — set ~0.5–1 A
~6 Ω / 25 W power resistorWorks, less friendly
Use an 1156 (bayonet base) or a 3156 (wedge base), or on a 1157/3157 use the bright filament contact. A 1157's dim filament is only ~8 W — much higher resistance, so it limits current harder than the FSM's 21 W and the valve may barely stroke. It won't damage anything, it just gives a weak/inconclusive test.
Must be a real incandescent bulb. An LED "replacement" has driver circuitry inside and will not behave as a current limiter.
Power source: any clean 12 V DC source works — car battery, lawnmower/motorcycle battery, a 12 V 7 Ah sealed battery, a 12 V jump pack, or a regulated bench supply. The bulb already limits current to about 1 A, so the source's amp capability is irrelevant. What matters is that it reads ~12.4–12.6 V at rest. Not a battery charger (unregulated, often 14 V+ and noisy), and not an 18 V/20 V tool battery. Verify 12 V with the meter before you start — some older mower batteries are 6 V.

A reading taken while on the charger means nothing. 13.2–13.9 V is normal charging/float voltage, so "13.5 V" or "13.9 V" on the charger is just the charger's output, not the battery's state. Take it off, let it rest ~30 min (surface charge bleeds off), then read it: 12.6–12.8 V = fully charged · ~12.4 V = ~75% · ~12.2 V = ~50%. For this test even 12.0 V is plenty.
Never jumper the solenoid straight to the battery. That's the one mistake that turns a good donor part into a ruined one.
FSM wording, verbatim
"Connect the positive (+) battery lead with a 21 W bulb to terminal 2 and the negative (−) battery lead to terminal 1 of the solenoid valve connector, and check the operation of the valve."

NOTICE: "While using the battery during the inspection, do not bring the positive and negative tester probes too close to each other as a short circuit may occur."

OK: "The solenoid makes an operating sound."
Two-terminal vs one-terminal solenoids

SL1 / SL2 / SLT have two pins — the coil sits between them. Test 1↔2, expect 5.0–5.6 Ω.

DSL (lock-up / torque converter clutch) and S4 have only one pin — the coil grounds through the solenoid body. That single terminal is correct, not a broken connector.

SolenoidTestSpec @ 20 °C
SL1 · SL2 · SLTterminal 1 ↔ terminal 25.0 – 5.6 Ω
DSL (lock-up)terminal ↔ solenoid body11 – 13 Ω
S4terminal ↔ solenoid body11 – 15 Ω

DSL and S4 need no bulb. The bulb exists only to drag the ~5 Ω solenoids down to about 1 A. A ~12 Ω coil already does that by itself — straight across 12.6 V it draws ~1.05 A, the same place the bulb lands SLT. So connect battery + to the terminal and battery − to the body, tap, and listen for the click.

The body must be bare metal — the case carries the ground return. Clip to clean metal, not paint or plastic. A bad body contact reads as a false open circuit.
Before you start
  • Car on ramps or stands, level — you'll need level for the refill later
  • Engine off, cool enough to work under
  • Drain pan positioned before you crack the plug
  • This will be messy. ATF comes out hot and spreads.
Getting to the valve body
1
Drain the ATF
Remove the drain plug — 49 Nm (36 ft-lbf) on reinstall. Expect about 3.7 qt. Measure what comes out.
2
Drop the pan
18 bolts. Loosen opposite corners progressively and keep a pan under the last corner — the pan holds residual fluid.
Bolts are only 7.8 Nm (69 in-lbf). They're small and easy to strip. Hand-snug plus a light torque check.
3
Clean the pan and magnet
Wipe the magnet. Read the debris: fine grey paste is normal wear. Chunks, brass-colored flakes, or a lot of clutch material is not — that changes the diagnosis.
4
Remove the oil strainer / filter
Bolts at 11 Nm (8 ft-lbf). Fit the new filter 35330-06010.
5
Now you're looking at the valve body
This is where the solenoids live: they're right there on the valve body, plug-and-play once exposed. The only trick is identifying the correct one — there are five and they look alike.
Identify SLT before you touch anything

The solenoids are physically similar and the connectors are the giveaway. Use the wiring difference:

  • SL1, SL2, SLT — two-pin connectors. Measure 1↔2.
  • DSL, S4 — single pin, grounded through the case.
If you have a loose donor solenoid on the bench, match it visually to the one on the valve body before removing anything. Visual matching is more reliable than counting positions.
Swapping the solenoid
1
Test both parts first
Resistance + bulb test on the incoming donor solenoid and on the installed solenoid. Decide which is healthier before anything goes back in.
2
Remove the old SLT
Unplug the connector, remove the retaining bolt, pull the solenoid straight out. Watch for the O-ring and any lock plate.
3
Fit the donor
Light coat of fresh ATF WS on the O-ring. Slide straight in — no twisting, no force. If it doesn't seat easily, something is misaligned.
4
Torque the retainer
6.6 Nm (67 kgf-cm, 58 in-lbf). That's barely snug — it's an inch-pound value, not foot-pounds. This is the easiest fastener on the job to over-torque.
Do not confuse this with the 11 Nm (8 ft-lbf) used on the other solenoids and the oil strainer. SLT specifically is the lighter value.
5
Reconnect the pigtail
Positive click, no strain on the wire. Check the harness for chafing while you're in there — a damaged wire causes the same symptoms as a bad solenoid.
Button it up
6
Valve body bolts (if you loosened any)
11 Nm (110 kgf-cm, 8 ft-lbf) — valve body to transaxle.
7
New pan gasket + pan
Clean mating surfaces — no old gasket residue, no RTV unless the FSM calls for it. 18 bolts at 7.8 Nm (69 in-lbf), working opposite corners in stages.
8
Drain plug
49 Nm (36 ft-lbf). New crush washer if you have one.

Level check — FSM procedure
1
Drive it to temperature
Fluid must reach 70–80 °C (158–176 °F). This is a real drive, not an idle warm-up.
2
Level surface, parking brake
Engine idling.
3
Cycle the shifter
Brake pedal depressed, shift P → L through every position, then back to P. This fills the circuits so the reading is honest.
4
Read the dipstick
Pull, wipe clean, push fully back in, pull again. Level must be in the HOT range.
Don't skip the recheck
The level check requires a hot drive, so the job does not end when the pan goes back on. Plan the sequence as: button up → drive to temp → check level → top off → check again. Don't put the car back in service until the level is confirmed HOT.
Two codes, one solenoid
CodeTypeMeaning
P2714PerformanceClutch slip detected — turbine vs. output speed mismatch
P2716ElectricalOpen or short in the SLT circuit

Both point at Shift Solenoid Valve SLT, the line-pressure (EPC) solenoid. Yours is P2714.

What SLT actually does: it modulates line pressure against throttle angle so shifts feel smooth. When SLT sticks on, line pressure drops and clutch engagement force falls off — which is why the ECM sees slip and sets a performance code rather than a circuit code.
FSM diagnostic path
1
Check for other DTCs first
Anything besides P2714 — chase that first. Multiple codes change the story.
2
Inspect SLT
Resistance + bulb test. Fails → replace. Passes → keep going.
3
Inspect the valve body
Look for foreign material on the valves. Contamination → repair or replace the valve body.
4
Inspect the torque converter clutch
If everything above passes, the FSM points to the transaxle assembly itself.
Expectations going in
  • ~70% a clean fluid + filter service resolves it
  • ~30% the solenoid genuinely needs replacing

You're doing both at once, which sidesteps the gamble — but it also means if the code returns, you'll want to know which change did what. Note the fluid condition and debris in the pan when you're in there. That tells you whether this was dirty fluid or a failing part.

If P2714 returns after all this, the remaining suspects are the valve body and the torque converter clutch. That's no longer a pan-off driveway job — it's a conversation about the transaxle.
Shakedown
  • Clear the code after the service
  • Drive normally at first — around-town, light throttle
  • Then reproduce the original condition: hard acceleration. That's the only test that matters, since that's when it tripped before.
  • Re-scan after. Clean means you're done.
An intermittent fault that only appears under load will not show up in a gentle test drive. You have to load it to prove it's fixed.
